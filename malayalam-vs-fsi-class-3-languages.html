Malayalam and the Class 3 languages

Languages with significant linguistic and cultural differences from English — and how much of Malayalam's grammar each one already gives you.

Home › Resources › Class 3 languages
Class 3 — 44 weeks · 1,100 hours. Languages with significant linguistic and cultural differences from English, according to the US Foreign Service Institute's published difficulty categories. This is Malayalam's own tier, and where the overlap becomes dramatic. Tamil scores a perfect ten; Sinhala nearly matches it; Turkish, Mongolian and Burmese all clear eight.

Shared grammatical features with Malayalam

Each language scored against the same ten features used throughout this site. A full match counts 1, a partial match 0.5.

Tamil 10 / 10

Malayalam's parent. Perfect match on all ten — they separated between the 9th and 13th centuries CE.

Sinhala 9.5 / 10

The surprise of this entire list. Indo-Aryan by ancestry, but centuries beside Tamil gave it almost every Dravidian feature Malayalam has.

Turkish 9 / 10

The textbook agglutinative language. Also the clearest case of grammatical evidentiality — the job Malayalam's ennu does.

Mongolian 9 / 10

Verb-final, case-marking, suffix-stacking, prenominal relatives. Far closer to Malayalam than any European language.

Burmese 8 / 10

Verb-final with postpositions, particles for questions, and a full respect system.

Hungarian 8 / 10

Eighteen cases and heavy suffix-stacking; one of the great agglutinative designs.

Hindi 7.5 / 10

Shares the dative subject exactly — mujhe thand lag rahi hai — plus verb-final order and the light verb karna.

Urdu 7.5 / 10

Structurally Hindi. Also the source, with Arabic, of much Mappila Malayalam vocabulary.

Finnish 7.5 / 10

Around fifteen cases against Malayalam's seven, plus agglutination and phonemic length — but not verb-final.

Bengali 7.5 / 10

Verb-final, postpositional, with a real dative subject and a three-way respect system.

Amharic 7.5 / 10

Semitic, but verb-final and agglutinative — and its script is an abugida like Malayalam's.

Nepali 7 / 10

Verb-final Indo-Aryan with dative experiencers and layered honorifics.

Georgian 6.5 / 10

Agglutinative with a rich case system and genuine dative-subject verbs.

Estonian 6.5 / 10

Fourteen cases, agglutination and three-way consonant length.

Persian 5.5 / 10

Verb-final and pro-drop — but prepositional, not postpositional, which breaks the usual pattern.

Tagalog 4.5 / 10

Agglutinative and famous for code-mixing — Taglish is Manglish's cousin.

Polish 4.5 / 10

Seven cases and dative experiencers; no verb-final order.

Russian 4 / 10

Мне холодно is a dative subject. Russian also drops the present-tense “to be”, exactly as Malayalam drops aanu.

Greek 4 / 10

Case and pro-drop — and the katharevousa/dimotiki split was one of Ferguson's founding diglossia cases, like Malayalam's own.

Thai 3 / 10

Pro-drop, question particles and elaborate respect — but isolating, not agglutinative.

Vietnamese 3 / 10

As Thai: particles and honorifics without any suffix machinery.

Icelandic 3 / 10

One of the few European languages that still builds experiencers the Malayalam way.

Hebrew 2.5 / 10

Pro-drop and gemination; a loan source in Kerala via the Cochin Jewish community.
