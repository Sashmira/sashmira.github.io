The pattern nobody points out

The US Foreign Service Institute sorts languages by how many hours an English speaker needs to reach professional working proficiency. Run every major language in those classes against Malayalam's ten core grammatical features, and something odd appears.

The harder a language is for an English speaker, the more likely it is to be built like Malayalam. Class 1 — the “easy” Romance and Germanic languages — average under two features shared. Class 4, the languages rated hardest of all, contain the closest structural relative Malayalam has outside its own family.

Browse by class

Class 1 — 24–30 weeks

Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Dutch, Swedish, Norwegian, Danish, Afrikaans. The easiest for English speakers, and the furthest from Malayalam.

Class 2 — 30–36 weeks

German, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili, Haitian Creole. German is the strongest European match Malayalam has.

Class 3 — 44 weeks

Tamil, Sinhala, Turkish, Hindi, Urdu, Finnish, Bengali, Mongolian, Burmese, Hungarian, Persian, Russian, Greek and more. Malayalam's own tier.

Class 4 — 88 weeks

Japanese, Korean, Mandarin, Cantonese, Arabic. Rated hardest of all — and home to Malayalam's closest non-Dravidian twin.

A note on the classification. The FSI publishes these categories for its own diplomats, and different sources split the top tier into four or five classes. Malayalam itself does not appear on the published list; it is placed here with Tamil, its parent language, in the 44-week tier. The hour counts describe difficulty for English speakers — they are not a ranking of the languages themselves.
