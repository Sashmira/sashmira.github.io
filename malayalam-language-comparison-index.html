Every language named in the course

Thirty-six languages, from Malayalam's parent to the Nordic languages that share its retroflex consonants. Each entry says what the relationship actually is — ancestry, contact, vocabulary, or pure structural coincidence.

LanguageRelationshipWhat it shares with Malayalam
Tamil
Dravidian		 Parent languageMalayalam separated from Old Tamil between the 9th and 13th centuries CE. Shares all ten core features: verb-final order, postpositions, agglutination, case endings, pro-drop, dative subject, the relative participle, question particles, geminate consonants and respect levels. Also shares the rare retroflex approximant — Tamil ழ to Malayalam ഴ — and is itself a textbook case of diglossia.
Sanskrit
Indo-Aryan		 Largest vocabulary donorA heavy superstrate absorbed as Malayalam diverged from Tamil. Loans come in two layers linguists label tatsama (taken unchanged) and tadbhava (reshaped to Malayalam sounds). The medieval literary style that fused the two was called Manipravalam — “ruby-coral”.
English
Germanic		 Contrast language & living donorThe language this course teaches against. Shares none of Malayalam's ten core features — its SVO order is the minority habit worldwide. Yet far more English than Hindi sits inside everyday spoken Malayalam; the mix is called Manglish, and borrowed nouns inflect like native ones (busil, skoolilekku, officil).
Japanese
Japonic		 Closest structural twinMatches Malayalam on all ten features despite no historical connection to Kerala. Verb-final, agglutinative, pro-drop, dative subject, relative participle. Question particle ka works exactly like Malayalam's -o; the copula desu mirrors aanu; the light verb suru mirrors cheyyuka; conditionals -tara/-eba mirror -aal/enkil; the small っ marks geminates; keigo is an elaborate version of the respect ladder.
Korean
Koreanic		 Very close structural matchNine of ten features. Verb-final, agglutinative, case-marking, pro-drop, relative participle. The copula -ida parallels aanu, the conditional -myeon parallels enkil, and Korean speech levels parallel nee / ningal / thaankal. Korean kinship terminology is comparably precise to Malayalam's.
Turkish
Turkic		 The textbook agglutinative languageNine of ten features. The clearest illustration of stacking separable suffixes on a stem. Marks yes/no questions with the particle mi, has a dedicated habitual (-er/-ir), and is the strongest example of grammatical evidentiality (the -miş past) — the same job Malayalam's ennu does.
Hindi
Indo-Aryan		 Shared South Asian grammarVerb-final, postpositional, case-marking, pro-drop. Uses the dative subject exactly as Malayalam does — mujhe thand lag rahi hai against enikku thanukkunnu. Uses the light verb karna like cheyyuka. Also forms causatives by suffix. Note: less Hindi than English sits inside spoken Malayalam.
Finnish
Uralic		 Agglutination and case, without verb-final orderAround fifteen cases against Malayalam's seven. Agglutinative, postpositional, geminate-marking, with the question particle -ko. Does not put the verb last, which is why it scores 7.5 rather than 9.
Arabic
Semitic		 Diglossia model & vocabulary donorFerguson's classic diglossia case — Modern Standard against Egyptian, Levantine or Gulf — the closest parallel to Malayalam's granthabhasha / sambhashabhasha split. Pro-drop and phonemic gemination. Also drops the present-tense “to be”, like Malayalam's dropped aanu. A major loan source, especially in Mappila speech.
Spanish
Romance		 Pro-drop and the respect ladderShares pro-drop and the tú/usted T–V distinction. Uses dental t/n like Malayalam's dental series. Its “personal a” before human objects parallels Malayalam's marking of specific animate objects. Otherwise structurally distant — 2.5 of 10.
Italian
Romance		 Gemination, clearly demonstratedfato “fate” against fatto “done” is the cleanest illustration of meaningful consonant length outside Malayalam's own kili/killi. Also pro-drop, and uses dental t/n.
Latin
Italic		 Case and verb-final orderA rich case system and Subject-Object-Verb order — the reminder that verb-final grammar is neither exotic nor modern. Also a loan source in Kerala's Christian speech.
Russian
Slavic		 Case system and the zero copulaRich case marking, and routinely drops the present-tense “to be” — the same behaviour as Malayalam's dropped aanu in fast speech.
German
Germanic		 Case systemFour cases — the familiar European entry point to understanding vibhakti, Malayalam's seven-role noun marking.
Hungarian
Uralic		 AgglutinationOne of the great agglutinative designs, alongside Turkish and Finnish — the same beads-on-a-thread logic Malayalam uses.
Basque
Isolate		 Agglutination and verb-final orderA language isolate that independently arrived at both suffix-stacking and SOV order — evidence that Malayalam's design reflects a common solution, not an accident of ancestry.
Swahili
Niger-Congo		 AgglutinationBuilds words by stacking separable affixes, exactly as Malayalam does — from an entirely unrelated family on another continent.
Icelandic
Germanic		 The dative subjectOne of the few European languages that still builds experiencers the Malayalam way — feelings happen to you, not by you.
Mandarin Chinese
Sino-Tibetan		 Retroflex consonantsIts retroflex series is made the same way as Malayalam's ട ഡ ണ ള റ — tongue tip curled back. Chinese kinship terminology is also comparably precise to Malayalam's.
Swedish
Germanic		 Retroflex consonants — the surpriseProduces retroflex sounds in clusters like rt and rd. Most learners are astonished that a Nordic language shares this with Malayalam.
Norwegian
Germanic		 Retroflex consonants — the surpriseSame as Swedish: retroflexion in rt/rd clusters, arrived at independently.
Quechua
Quechuan		 EvidentialityAmong the world's strongest grammatical evidentiality systems — marking how you know something, the job Malayalam gives to ennu.
Bulgarian
Slavic		 EvidentialityGrammatically marks reported versus witnessed information, like Turkish -miş and Malayalam ennu.
Tibetan
Sino-Tibetan		 EvidentialityAnother language where the source of your knowledge is built into the grammar rather than added in words.
Greek
Hellenic		 Diglossia modelThe katharevousa versus dimotiki split was one of Ferguson's founding diglossia cases — the same high/low structure Malayalam has.
Swiss German
Germanic		 Diglossia modelFerguson's third classic case: a written standard nobody speaks alongside a spoken variety nobody writes.
French
Romance		 The respect ladderThe tu/vous choice described by Brown and Gilman as the T–V distinction — the same decision Malayalam forces with nee, ningal and thaankal.
Persian
Iranian		 Vocabulary donorLoanwords absorbed through centuries of Malabar coast trade, especially into Mappila speech.
Portuguese
Romance		 Vocabulary donorHousehold vocabulary left behind by the colonial trading centuries; especially present in Christian community speech.
Dutch
Germanic		 Vocabulary donorA second colonial-era loan layer in Kerala's everyday vocabulary.
Hebrew
Semitic		 Vocabulary donor & alternative scriptLoans via the Cochin Jewish community, one of Kerala's oldest — which also wrote Malayalam in a Hebrew-based script.
Syriac
Semitic		 Vocabulary donorLoans via the Saint Thomas Christian community, among the oldest Christian communities anywhere.
Urdu
Indo-Aryan		 Community vocabulary & the light verbMuslim (Mappila) speech borrows heavily from Arabic and Urdu. Urdu also uses karna as a light verb exactly as Malayalam uses cheyyuka and Japanese uses suru.
Kannada
Dravidian		 Northern dialect influenceKerala's northern dialects — Kasaragod and Malabar — lean toward Kannada, producing forms like bili, bere and baa that a Travancore speaker would not use.
Gujarati
Indo-Aryan		 The nationality suffixIllustrates Malayalam's completely regular place-of-origin suffix: gujaraattikkaaran (male), gujaraattikkaari (female), gujaraattikkaar (plural). Build it, never look it up.
Tagalog
Austronesian		 Code-mixing parallelEnglish–Tagalog blending produces Taglish, exactly as Malayalam–English blending produces Manglish — and Spanish–English produces Spanglish.
Ethiopic (Ge'ez)
Semitic		 Script familyThe Malayalam script is a Brahmic abugida, kin to Devanagari and the Tamil script — and, further afield, to Ethiopic, which uses the same consonant-plus-inherent-vowel principle.
The pattern worth noticing. Japanese, Korean and Turkish score higher on shared grammar than Spanish does — and far higher than English — despite having no historical connection to Kerala whatsoever.
